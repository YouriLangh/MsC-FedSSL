## 1. Positioning: what you’ve actually learned

Your constraints were:

- **No changes to the federated optimization protocol** (FedAvg only)
    
- Only **lightweight, local SSL-style tweaks**:
    
    - adaptive thresholds (ADSH-style)
        
    - short-term pseudo-label retention (STM)
        

CBAFed, in contrast, gets its gains from **much heavier changes**:

- fixed pseudo-labeling to avoid forgetting
    
- server-side **class-balanced thresholds** using global statistics
    
- tail-class discovery
    
- residual weight connections in local and global training
    

So if your ADSH + STM variants **don’t improve FSSL**, that’s not a failure; it’s actually an important conclusion:

> _“Local, centrally-inspired adaptive thresholding and short-term pseudo-label retention are **not sufficient** to fix FixMatch+FedAvg under strong non-IID + label scarcity. Methods like CBAFed likely rely on their heavier global components (fixed PL, server-side thresholds, residual aggregation), not just ‘adaptive thresholds’ in the abstract.”_

Your RQ2 already allows this:

> _“To what extent do adaptive thresholding and pseudo-label retention improve convergence and accuracy compared to the baseline?”_

You can legitimately answer:

- “They **don’t** help in most regimes,” and
    
- “Here is a detailed diagnosis of _why_, based on utilization, pseudo-label noise, and forgetting.”
    

That’s a solid thesis result.

### (C) Diagnostic analysis: _why_ they don’t help

This is the “meat” that makes the null result interesting.

Tie this to your two hypotheses:

> (A) Low data utilization and pseudo-label bias due to fixed τ  
> (B) Catastrophic forgetting amplifying model drift

Then show diagnostics for baseline vs your variants.

#### For (A) “data utilization & pseudo-label bias”

Run + plot:

1. **Pseudo-label utilization per round**
    
    - Fraction of unlabeled samples that pass the threshold:
        
        - global fraction
            
        - per-class fraction (for a few rounds)
            
    - Compare:
        
        - Fixed τ (baseline)
            
        - ADSH thresholds
            
    - You’ll likely see:
        
        - Under heavy non-IID, ADSH doesn’t dramatically increase usable samples, or it increases them mostly for majority classes.
            
2. **Pseudo-label accuracy vs round**
    
    - On a small _labeled_ validation subset, simulate pseudo-labeling and compute:
        
        - accuracy of pseudo-labels per class and overall
            
    - Compare baseline vs ADSH:
        
        - If pseudo-labels are still noisy/bias-prone, you can say: “Threshold adaptation alone doesn’t improve pseudo-label _quality_ in FSSL.”
            
3. **Class coverage**
    
    - For each client and each round:
        
        - How many classes actually get used in the unsupervised loss?
            
    - Show that even with ADSH, many clients rarely contribute minority classes, because their local data simply doesn’t contain them.
        

These diagnostics let you say:

> “In FSSL, the main issue is not just the global value of τ but the interaction between local non-IID data and pseudo-label noise; ADSH does not meaningfully fix this.”

---

#### For (B) “catastrophic forgetting”

Here you want evidence that:

- Models forget knowledge from labeled clients as they train on unlabeled, non-IID clients
    
- STM retention doesn’t really prevent this
    

Possible tests:

1. **Global validation accuracy after each phase**
    
    - Evaluate on a fixed IID validation set:
        
        - After labeled-only rounds (warm-up)
            
        - After each unlabeled training phase
            
    - Compare the curve:
        
        - Baseline vs STM
            
    - If STM doesn’t “preserve” early accuracy better, you can say it doesn’t mitigate forgetting in practice.
        
2. **Client-specific forgetting**
    
    - Take a subset of labeled data for each client (or a synthetic per-client validation split).
        
    - After several rounds:
        
        - Evaluate the global model’s accuracy on:
            
            - Client 1’s validation
                
            - Client 2’s validation
                
            - … etc.
                
    - See if performance on “older” clients decays.
        
3. **Representation drift**
    
    - Track cosine similarity or CKA between:
        
        - the representation layer after warm-up and after later rounds
            
    - If both baseline and STM drift similarly, STM isn’t stabilizing the representation.
        

If you don’t have time to do everything, **(1)** (global validation vs round) is the most important.

## How I’d structure it when there’s no improvement

### 1. Always keep baseline vs FixFed in the main results

For each main scenario (dataset, α, label budget), still show:

- **Baseline (FixMatch + FedAvg)**
    
- **FixFed full (ADSH + STM)**
    

Even if lines overlap, that answers RQ2 directly:

> “Across all tested conditions, FixFed does not systematically improve accuracy or convergence over the baseline.”

You can then discuss _why_ using the diagnostics we talked about.

---

### 2. Be selective with _ablation depth_, but still do it

You don’t have to explode the paper with 100 plots, but you **should** include at least one or two ablation tables/figures:

- Baseline
    
- - adaptive thresholding only (ADSH)
        
- - STM only
        
- - ADSH + STM (FixFed)
        

On **one representative setup per dataset** (e.g. α=0.5, 100 labels/class), that’s enough to show:

- “No single component gives consistent gains.”
    
- “Sometimes they even slightly hurt.”
    

That supports the statement:

> “Simply adding adaptive thresholds or label retention, without changing the federated optimization, is not enough to fix FixMatch+FedAvg.”

---

### 3. Shift emphasis from “who wins” to “what changes”

Because there’s no accuracy gain, your ablations should be used more for **behavioural insight** than leaderboard scores:

For each ablation, instead of just:

> “Accuracy: 74.2 vs 74.0 vs 73.9 → nothing happens.”

also show things like:

- pseudo-label utilization (more/less samples selected)
    
- pseudo-label accuracy
    
- per-class coverage
    
- stability of training curves
    

Then you can say things like:

> “Adaptive thresholding increases the number of selected pseudo-labels by ~15%, but the additional labels are too noisy and do not translate into better test performance.”

That’s a _good_ negative result.

If time/space is tight, you can:
